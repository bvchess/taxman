Chapter 4

Frames

Once greedy has chosen the numbers above n/2, the board splits into frames, small games that barely touch. Only the promotions are left to choose.

Splitting the board

The frame lemmaLemma F1proved, proof on GitHub

Every number lies in exactly one frame. A frame's sources share a layer, and its factors lie in the layer just below.

Precisely.The frame graph of game n joins each source to its maximal factors, and a frame is one of its connected components. For n ≥ 2, each frame's sources lie in one layer k and its factors in layer k − 1, and a frame in front of it lies in layer k − 1.

Chapter 3 left game 21 in its layers, with its sources chosen: U* in gray, and 11, 13 and 17 rejected. Now gather each source with the factors that can pay for it, and the board falls apart into four groups. Each is a frame: some sources with every factor that can pay for one of them.

Every number lies in exactly one frame. A frame's sources share a layer, and its factors lie in the layer just below. One frame is in front of another when a number of the other has a maximal factor in it, as 9 has 3. The frame in front stands to the right.

The layer theoremTheorem F3proved, proof on GitHub

A set can be played exactly when the moves of each layer can be paid for by the factors one layer down that the set does not play.

Precisely.S ⊆ {2, …, n} is playable iff each of its layer instances is: at layer k, the members Sk against the numbers of layer k − 1 up to n/2 that are not in S.

Each frame is a small game of its own: its sources pay only with its own factors. Two frames meet only where a factor of one is played as a move. That takes it away from its own frame and makes it one more move of the frame in front, where its maximal factors are.

Freedoms

The winnability lemmaLemma F5proved, proof on GitHub

A frame's own game can be won exactly when its moves can be paid for, so a frame never has fewer factors than moves.

Precisely.A pair W of moves in one layer and factors in the layer below can be won, every move taken while it has a maximal factor left, iff W read as an instance is playable.

Freedoms, precisely

A frame D with the factors FD has |FD| − |U* ∩ D| freedoms, and is closed when it has none. In a game that plays all of U*, a closed frame's factors pay exactly its moves (the closed-frames lemma).

In each frame, the sources that greedy kept are its moves, and each needs a factor of its own to pay for it. A frame's freedoms are its factors less its moves: how many factors it can spare. Count them. The two frames at the ends have as many factors as moves. They are closed: 8 pays for 16 and 1 for 19, and nothing in them is left to choose. The other two have four factors and three moves, one freedom each. The frames with freedoms are numbered from the front: frame 0 at the right, with the sources 14, 15 and 21, and frame 1 behind it, with 12, 18 and 20.

Promotions

A factor that its frame can spare can be played as a move. To promote a factor is to play it: it leaves its frame, which no longer needs it, and becomes one more move of the frame in front, where one of its own maximal factors pays for it. A candidate is a factor that its frame can spare and that the frame in front can take on. In game 21 the candidates are 4 and 9, drawn with a second outline, and a dotted line runs from each to its maximal factor in the frame in front.

Why not 10, or 6? Either would close a loop in frame 0, and the numbers of a loop cannot all be played: 10 would make chapter 1's loop with 14, 21 and 15, and 6 a smaller one with 14 and 21. But 9 has a single maximal factor, 3, so it closes no loop, and it fits. So does 4.

The promotion-limit lemmaLemma F9proved, proof on GitHub

Each promotion spends a freedom of the frame it leaves and one of the frame it joins, so the freedoms cap how many promotions fit.

Precisely.With ρh freedoms and κh candidates in frame h, numbered from the front, no promotion set has more members than the largest c1 + ⋯ + ct with 0 ≤ ch ≤ κh and ch + ch+1 ≤ ρh. In game 21 it is 1.

Only one of the two can go, since each frame here has one freedom and a promotion spends one in each. Promote 9, the larger. It hops to the right, from frame 1 into frame 0, where 3 pays for it, and the score is 135 + 9 = 144.

The frames also give the order of play: the frames in front first, from the right. Play the game: the moves are 19, 9, 15, 21, 14, 18, 12, 20 and 16, each taking the one tax its thick line marks. Then no move is left, and the taxman takes 11, 13 and 17. The game ends 144 to 87, and 144 is opt(21).

The frame model

So once U* is chosen, a game is settled by choosing the candidates to promote together. A set of candidates that can all be promoted together is a promotion set, and an optimal game requires the heaviest one.

The exactness theoremTheorem X7proved, proof on GitHub

The promotion sets are exactly the sets of numbers up to n/2 that a game playing all of U* can add.

Precisely.In every root problem of game n, U* ∪ L is playable iff L is a promotion set. So ΣU* + P*, with P* the weight of the heaviest promotion set, is the largest weight of a playable set that contains U*.

The corollary of statement UCorollary X8proved, proof on GitHub

The frame model's best score is at most opt(n), and equal to it exactly where statement U holds.

Precisely.ΣU* + P* ≤ opt(n), with equality iff statement U holds at n. The chain's scores past game 2,530 are proven within the frame model, so they are opt(n) given statement U (what supports the scores).

The frame model fixes the sources to U* and leaves only the promotions to choose. That loses nothing for a game that plays all of U*: below n/2, such a game can add any promotion set, and nothing else. So the frame model's best score is the best score of any game that plays all of U*. Statement U, from chapter 3, says that some optimal game does. Wherever it holds, the frame model's best score is opt(n), the best score of the game itself.

How much is left

The frame model in the code

The frame solver forms the frames, keeps U* and finds the candidates in its setup, then searches the promotion sets (the design; solver/frame/src/frames).

Game 21 has two candidates. Game 100 has 17, in frames 1 to 3.

Game 100’s optimal game promotes 4 of its candidates, 44, 45, 49 and 50, and they carry 188 of its 3,164 points: about 6% of the score. The other 94% is U*, which one greedy pass chose. Each candidate is promoted or not, so the 17 allow 217 = 131,072 sets, and only 1,042 of them are promotion sets: every other set breaks some frame. Which promotion set is the heaviest is the one choice left, and a search makes it.

One optimal set to game 424measured, evidence on GitHub

Every game up to 424 has one optimal set, and game 425 has two.

Precisely.The prover lists every optimal set of the games it proved. There is one in every game n ≤ 424; from game 425 on a few games have two or three. In every game listed, the optimal sets share U* and their size, and differ by an exchange of equal sum below n/2. Which one a run records depends on the order of its search (games with several optimal sets).

The relaxation propositionProposition Q2proved, proof on GitHub

A game with two optimal sets has no greedy shortcut to its best score.

Precisely.In a game with two or more optimal sets, such as game 425, every family of subsets of 2..n that is closed under subsets, holds every playable set, and on which the greedy algorithm finds a heaviest member for every nonnegative weighting has a member that weighs more than opt(n).

Along the way: game 425 OpenClose

Every game up to 424 has one optimal set of moves. Game 425 has two. They play the same numbers above n/2, U*, and the same 20 promotions, and differ in three more: one plays 168, 132 and 110, the other 162, 150 and 98.

Both threes sum to 410, so a game that plays either set scores 57,010, the best possible, and they trade one promotion into each of frames 2, 1 and 0. A tie like this rules out a shortcut like chapter 3's for the whole game. On a family of sets where a greedy pass always finds the heaviest, distinct numbers give only one heaviest set, and game 425 has two.
