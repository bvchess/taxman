The story

Optimal games of Taxman

We found an optimal game of Taxman for every n from 1 to the end of the chain. This essay explains how, from the rules up. Its first four chapters are written: they take the board of a game apart into frames, small games that hardly touch each other, and show the one choice that is left.

The game

Taxman is a game for one player, played against a taxman on the numbers 1 to n. The numbers start in the pot.

  • You take a number from the pot, and score it. That number is your move.
  • The taxman then takes every divisor of your move still in the pot: its tax.
  • You may take only a number that has a divisor left in the pot. The taxman must always get something.
  • When no number you could take is left, the taxman takes the rest.
Notation

opt(n) is the best score of game n. The scores opt(1), opt(2), opt(3), … are the Taxman sequence, A019312 in the OEIS. A divisor here is always a proper one, below the move; the rules in full are in the glossary.

Your score is the sum of your moves. The best score anyone can make on 1 to n is written opt(n), and a game that scores it is an optimal game. Game 10, on the numbers 1 to 10, is a good start.

The prime lemmaLemma N1proved, proof on GitHub

Every optimal game opens with the largest prime on the board, and plays no other prime.

Precisely.Let n ≥ 2, and let q(n) be the largest prime up to n. Every optimal game of n plays q(n) as its first move, and plays no other prime.

The coins in the pot are the numbers above n/2, and the tiles are the rest. A coin divides no other number on the board, so the taxman never takes one as tax: he gets a coin only at the end, if you never took it. A number you may take stands up; one you may not lies flat.

One rule of thumb is always right: open with the largest prime on the board. It costs you only the 1, and a prime can only ever open a game, since the 1 is its one divisor.

Sets, not orders

What you score depends on which numbers you take, not on the order you take them in. The order matters only to keep each move legal, with a divisor still in the pot when you take it.

Playable, precisely

A set S ⊆ {2, …, n} is playable when some order of it is the moves of a legal game of n. Its weight is ΣS, and an optimal set is a playable set of weight opt(n). The theory decides playability by payment schemes.

One optimal set, to 424measured, evidence on GitHub

Up to game 424 every game has exactly one optimal set; from 425 on, a few have two or three.

Precisely.The prover enumerates the optimal sets of the games it proves. Game 425 has two, which trade {110, 132, 168} for {98, 150, 162}, each of sum 410.

So the question for game n is which sets of numbers can be played, in some order. We call such a set playable. The sum of a set is its weight, and an optimal set, the set of moves of an optimal game, is a heaviest playable set.

The lifting lemmaLemma P1proved, proof on GitHub

A number can be played exactly when one of its maximal factors is still in the pot.

Precisely.At any point of a game, if a proper divisor d of x is in the pot, so is a maximal factor f of x with d | f. The maximal factors of x are x/p, for each prime p dividing x.

Whether a move is legal depends only on its maximal factors: the number divided by one of its primes. 12 has the maximal factors 6 and 4, and 9 has only 3. If any divisor of a move is still in the pot, so is a maximal factor, so a move is legal exactly when one of its maximal factors is in the pot. That factor is the one that pays for the move. In the figure each move stands on the maximal factor that pays for it, and it is the same one in every order.

The length propositionProposition Q4proved, proof on GitHub

In game 833 every optimal game has 362 moves, and the longest games have 363.

Precisely.In game 833 = 7² · 17 a heaviest game of 363 moves scores 218,197, which is 29 less than opt(833) = 218,226. Proved by computation, with the prover and a maximum matching.

Along the way: game 833More moves, fewer points OpenClose

Taking more numbers does not always score more. In game 833, every optimal game makes 362 moves, yet some games make one more. The longest of those still scores less: they play five smaller numbers where the optimal game plays four larger ones.

The trade happens below n/2. Each of the nine numbers is a maximal factor of a source the optimal game plays, and a number taken as a move cannot also pay for a source. The optimal game takes the four large ones and pays for those sources some other way; the longest game leaves the four to pay and takes five smaller ones instead.

Layers and frames

A board is not one big game. It falls apart into small ones, frames, which touch each other only through a few numbers. To see how, we take the board of game 21 apart in one figure, a step at a time. The same 21 numbers move through every step, so you can follow any one of them.

The marks

Every figure on this site uses the same marks. The numbers you play stand up from the page, as raised tokens; the numbers in a figure that explains are drawn flat, in ink. The two share their shapes and their meanings.

Start with the pot, the numbers 1 to 21. The figure follows the words: the underlined ones move it, and so do the slider under it and the arrow keys. A number named in the text, such as 12, gets a halo in the figure when you point at it.

Layers, precisely

The layer of x is Ω(x), its count of prime factors with multiplicity: Ω(12) = Ω(2 · 2 · 3) = 3, and Ω(1) = 0. Each maximal factor x/p lies in layer Ω(x) − 1, so every line of the maximal factor graph joins two neighboring layers.

Every number has a layer: its count of prime factors, with repeats. 12 = 2 · 2 · 3 is in layer 3, a prime is in layer 1, and 1 alone is in layer 0. When we set the numbers out in layers, a column each with layer 1 at the right, and join each one to its maximal factors, every line runs one column to the right, toward the front: 12 to 6 and 4, and every prime to 1.

Sources, precisely

The sources of game n are the s with n/2 < s ≤ n. Since 2s > n, no multiple of s but s itself is on the board, so s is never tax. The factors are 1 to ⌊n/2⌋.

Next we cut every column at n/2, factors above the line and sources below it. The numbers above n/2, 11 to 21 here, are the sources. A source divides no other number on the board, so it is never tax: it is a move or nothing. The numbers up to n/2 are the factors. Each is tax for some larger number, and sometimes a move itself. The lines that matter from here on are the ones from a source to a factor.

The frame lemmaLemma F1proved, proof on GitHub

Every number lies in exactly one frame. A frame's sources share a layer, and its factors lie in the layer just below.

Precisely.Let n ≥ 2. The frame graph of game n joins each source to its maximal factors, and a frame is one of its connected components. Every number of the board lies in exactly one frame. In each frame the sources all lie in one layer k and the factors in layer k − 1. A frame in front of a frame of layer k lies in layer k − 1.

Then we group the numbers into frames. A frame is a group of sources that share factors, together with every factor that can pay for them. Game 21 has four frames, one a layer. They sit side by side, the frame in front always to the right, each with its sources in a column at its left and its factors at its right. Every number lies in exactly one frame, a frame's sources share a layer, and its factors lie in the layer just below.

The bottom-layers theoremTheorem F4proved, proof on GitHub

In layers 1 and 2, every optimal game plays exactly the sources descending greedy keeps.

Precisely.Let n ≥ 2 and S an optimal set of n. Then S holds q(n) and no other prime, and the sources of S in layer 2 are exactly U*2, the members of U* in layer 2. U* is what descending greedy keeps of the sources, deciding each step by peel.

Which sources should we play? Descending greedy goes through the sources from the largest down and keeps each one that can still be played together with those it kept before. It keeps 21, 20 and 19, then rejects 17: 19, 17, 13 and 11 are primes, whose one maximal factor is 1, and 1 already pays for 19. The sources it keeps are called U*. Here they score 135, the score of the game that promotes nothing. Keeping them is a choice, and chapter 4 says what it costs.

Freedoms, precisely

A frame D with factors FD and kept sources KD has |FD| − |KD| freedoms, and is closed when it has none. In game 21 a candidate is a factor x of a frame with a freedom such that U* ∪ {x} is playable. The glossary has the solver's own, wider sense of the word.

Each frame is a small game of its own. Count each frame's freedoms: its factors less its moves. The frames of layers 1 and 4 have one factor and one move each. They are closed: every factor is needed to pay, and they drop out. The frames of layers 2 and 3 have four factors and three moves, so each has one factor to spare. Frame X is in front of frame Y when a number of Y has a maximal factor in X, as 9 has 3. A candidate is a factor that its frame can spare and that the frame in front can take on as one more move: here 4 and 9. The dotted arrows point from a factor to its maximal factor in the frame in front.

Promotions

Promotion sets

A promotion set is a set L of numbers up to n/2 such that U* ∪ L is playable. The frame solver's answer is ΣU* + P*, where P* is the largest ΣL; in game 21, L = {9} and 135 + 9 = 144. The search explores these sets, and the exactness theorem, below, says they are all there is.

To promote a factor is to play it as a move. When we promote 9, it makes a short hop to the right: it leaves the factors of the layer-3 frame and becomes a move of the layer-2 frame in front of it, paid for by 3. Only one candidate fits, since each frame had one factor to spare, and 9 is the larger. The score is 135, what U* scores, plus 9: 144.

This is what the frame solver searches: which candidates to promote together. Everything else is settled before its search begins. Game 21 has two candidates; larger games have hundreds, and a search settles which to promote.

The frames also give an order of play: frames in front first, from the right. When we play the game, the player takes 19, 9, 15, 21, 14, 18, 12, 20 and 16, each with the one tax its thick line marks. Then no move is left, and the taxman takes 11, 13 and 17. The game ends 144 to 87, and 144 is opt(21).

Why 9 and not 10: the loop

Why could the layer-2 frame take 9 on, and not 10? The numbers 10, 14, 15 and 21 have two prime factors each, so each is a line between two primes. Together they make a square on 2, 5, 3 and 7: 10 = 2 · 5, 15 = 5 · 3, 21 = 3 · 7 and 14 = 7 · 2. Play them, with only the four primes as tax.

The loop ruleProposition P11, the two-factor propositionproved, proof on GitHub

Numbers with two prime factors can all be played exactly when their lines form no loop.

Precisely.Let S ⊆ {2, …, n}, each member with at most two maximal factors outside S. Its graph has a vertex for each of those factors and one more, the ground, and an edge for each member: between its two factors, or from its one factor to the ground. Then S is playable if and only if the graph has no cycle, two members joining the same factor to the ground counting as one.

The theory draws the same square in cycle-21.svg.

Whatever the order, the first move takes both of its primes, and at least one of the four is stuck. Four numbers need four primes to pay with, one each, and the first move spends two. The rule: numbers with two prime factors can all be played, in some order, exactly when their lines form no loop.

In the layer-2 frame of game 21, 14, 21 and 15 already make three sides of the square, so 10 would close it, and 6 = 2 · 3 would close a triangle with 14 and 21. But 9 has a single maximal factor, 3. It hangs from one prime, closes nothing, and fits.

What the model costs

The exactness theoremTheorem X7proved, proof on GitHub

The frame model's best score is the best score of a game that plays all of U*.

Precisely.The promotion sets of every root problem of game n are exactly the sets L of numbers up to n/2 for which U* ∪ L is playable. So ΣU* + P* is the largest weight of a playable set that contains U*.

The frame model fixes the sources to U* and searches only the promotions. Is anything lost? The answer has two parts.

First, nothing is lost given U*: the best score of the frame model is the best score of any game that plays every source in U*.

Statement Uand corollary X8open, evidence on GitHub

Some optimal game plays every source that descending greedy keeps in layers 3 and up.

Precisely.U at n: some optimal set of n contains U*≥3, the members of U* in layers 3 and up. Corollary X8, proved: ΣU* + P* ≤ opt(n), with equality if and only if U holds at n. Layers 1 and 2 are settled by the bottom-layers theorem.

The chain's scoresproved given U, proof on GitHub

Every score of the chain is opt(n) wherever statement U holds at n.

Precisely.Each recorded score is ΣU* + P*, optimal in the frame model given that the solver is right, and so opt(n) by X8 given U at n. For the first games of the chain the prover, which knows nothing of frames, proves the scores on the whole board.
