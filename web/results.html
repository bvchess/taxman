Chapter 8

Results

For every game from game 1 to game the end of the chain, the chain records an optimal game and the rule or search behind its score. Together the games show where the player's share settles.

Every game

Choose any game of the chain. The explorer below shows what the chain records for it: what the player and the taxman each collect in an optimal game, what proves the score is optimal, how many promotions the optimal game makes, and what its search cost. For games up to 100 it also draws the moves on the board.

A game’s record, precisely

The record is the game’s line of the results file. Its in_the_bag is ΣU*, the score with no promotions.

One run made every line, from November 1 to 6, 2026, on one machine with 88 threads, and it worked on up to four games at once. A game’s time, seconds.active_all_runs, counts only the time that at least one thread worked on it, not the time it waited while the threads worked on other games (the frame solver’s reference; the run).

Most of each score comes from the top half: U*, the numbers above n/2 that descending greedy keeps. The promotions chosen by the search add the rest. The search grows steeply with n: in game 21 it explores 1 node, and in game 5,060 it explores 1.2 billion.

The player’s share

Hensley’s theoremHensley, 1988proved, the paper

As n grows, the player’s share tends to a limit: more than 50%, and at most 35/48, about 73%.

Precisely.The limit C of opt(n) / (n²/2) exists, and 1/2 < C ≤ 35/48: D. Hensley, “A winning strategy at Taxman”, The Fibonacci Quarterly 26 (1988) 262–270. The share has the same limit, since 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n = n(n + 1)/2 and (n + 1)/n tends to 1.

The player can win all but twoFranklín and Moniot, Theorem 5proved, the paper

The player can beat the taxman in all but games 1 and 3.

Precisely.Their born-free matching wins every game n ∉ {1, 3} but games 7 and 13, which they win by hand: A. F. Franklín and R. K. Moniot, Discrete Applied Mathematics 339 (2023) 166–171 (what the paper proves).

An optimal game wins a share of the board: its score, opt(n), divided by 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n, the total of the board. The player beats the taxman when the share is above one half. In every game of the chain except games 1 and 3, it is.

In 1988 Douglas Hensley proved that as n grows, the share tends to a limit: more than 50%, and at most 35/48, about 73%. So in the long run the taxman keeps more than a quarter of the board. The theorem does not say what the limit is.

The share near 63%measured, evidence on GitHub

From game 4,000 on, the share stays between 62.8% and 63.0%.

Precisely.The share opt(n) / (n(n + 1)/2) stays in that range in every game from game 4,000 to game 5060, by the scores of results/optimal.jsonl.gz. It measures finitely many games, and proves nothing about C.

The chain’s games put the limit near 63%. The share swings widely in the first games and settles as n grows: from game 4,000 on it stays between 62.8% and 63.0%, and game 5060 wins 62.9%. That is a measurement across a finite number of games, not a proof about the limit.

What the games show

One optimal set, to game 424measured, evidence on GitHub

Every game up to 424 has exactly one optimal set; game 425 has two.

Precisely.The prover lists every optimal set of 954 of the games 2 to 989, every game to 797 among them: one in every game up to 424, and two or three in 30 games from game 425 to game 779 (the evidence).

A game usually has just one optimal set. The prover has listed every optimal set of 954 of the games 2 to 989, and all but 30 of those have exactly one. Game 425 is the first with two. The two differ by an exchange: one plays 110, 132 and 168, the other 98, 150 and 162, and both trios sum to 410 (chapter 4).

So a game’s best score can be reached by more than one set of moves. Where a game has several optimal sets, the chain keeps the one its search found (the games with several).

How far an optimal game falls shortmeasured, evidence on GitHub

Up to game 4,949, every recorded optimal game makes the most moves its board allows, except in games 833, 834 and 835, where it makes one fewer.

Precisely.The prover settles ℓ(n), the most moves of a game of n, in games 2 to 4,949. Whether the shortfall is bounded is open.

Nor does an optimal game always make as many moves as it can. In game 833, every optimal game makes 362 moves, yet a game can make 363, and the best of those scores 29 less. Chapter 1 shows the trade.
