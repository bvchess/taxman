Playing optimal games of Taxman

Taxman is a number game played against a taxman. We play an optimal game, one with the best possible score, for every n from 1 to the end of our chain. This site explains how we did it.

The game

The rules, precisely

A move takes a number that still has a proper divisor in the pot, and the taxman takes all of its proper divisors still in the pot. When no move is left, the taxman takes the rest. The best score of game n is opt(n), and opt(1), opt(2), opt(3), … is the Taxman sequence, A019312 in the OEIS.

Taxman is played on the numbers 1 to n, which start in the pot.

  • You take a number from the pot and score it. That number is your move.
  • The taxman then takes every divisor of your move still in the pot: its tax.
  • You may take only a number that has a divisor left in the pot, so the taxman always gets something.
  • When no number you could take is left, the taxman takes the rest.
  • You beat the taxman when your total is higher than the total he collects.

The best total you can reach in game n is written opt(n), and a game that reaches it is an optimal game. Try game 10.

Heuristic strategies

A heuristic strategy plays a good game quickly, by rules such as taking the largest number that can still be taken, or the one that costs the least tax, without proving that the game is the best. Many have been proposed, and judging one needs the best score to measure it against. That is one reason to know opt(n). The repository holds our strategies and how they were measured, and Robert Moniot's seven.

The blue button plays an optimal game, the best you can do. For game 10 you can find one by hand. Playing well is easy even in large games: a simple strategy with no search comes within a fraction of a percent of the best score. But only a perfect game is optimal, and only a proof shows that a game is perfect.

How we did it

The counts, precisely

Rough sizes, not counts of legal games: n! orders, 2n subsets of the board, 2⌊n/2⌋ subsets of the numbers up to n/2, and 2c subsets of the c candidates. The last row is the nodes that the game's recorded search explored.

Game n's numbers can be put in about 10… orders. No computer could try them all, and we never try them. Each chapter of the story takes away part of the problem, until what is left is small enough for a search to finish.

The chain

How each game was settled

By a search of its whole tree; as a repeat of an earlier game's search, which poses the same problem; at its ceiling, n + opt(n − 1); by the prime formula; by an upper bound in the bounds store, which the referee checks against a game that meets it; or trivially, with nothing to choose. The proof kinds are defined in the frame solver's reference.

The player's share

The share is opt(n) / (1 + 2 + ⋯ + n). Hensley proved in 1988 that opt(n) / (n²/2) tends to a constant between 1/2 and 35/48. The chain's games put it near 0.63, which is a measurement, not a proof.

We found the games in order, from game 1 up, each one built from an optimal game of the one before, so they form a chain. The chart shows every game of it: the share of the board that the player wins, and how each game was settled.

Check it yourself

Checking the chain

From the top of the repository, after building the frame solver: taxman verify-results results/optimal.jsonl.gz, and the independent check in Python, python3 solver/tools/check_results.py results/optimal.jsonl.gz. Both replay every game.

What supports the scores

Every score up to 2175 is opt(n) on the whole board, and every score past it is opt(n) given statement U. Chapter 6 says what each one rests on.

Every game, with its score and its proof, is in one file, and two programs check it. The code, the theory and the record of what we tried are on GitHub.
