Taxman

Playing optimal games

Brian Chess

Taxman is a number game originally created to teach kids about factors. We play an optimal game, one with the best possible score, in each of games 1 to 5,060. This site explains how we did it.

In a hurry? Go straight to the summary.

The game

The rules, precisely

Each move the player makes takes a number that still has a proper divisor in the pot, then the taxman takes all of its divisors still in the pot. Each move must pay one or more divisors as tax. When no move is left, the taxman takes the rest. The best player score possible for game n is opt(n), and opt(1), opt(2), opt(3), … is the Taxman sequence, A019312 in the OEIS.

Taxman is played with the numbers 1 to n, which start in the pot.

  • You take a number from the pot and score it. That number is your move.
  • The taxman then takes every divisor of your move still in the pot: its tax.
  • You may take only a number that has a divisor left in the pot, so the taxman always gets something.
  • When no number you could take is left, the taxman takes the rest.
  • You beat the taxman when your total is higher than the total he collects.

The best total you can reach in game n is written opt(n), and a game that reaches it is an optimal game. Try game 10.

Heuristic strategies

A heuristic strategy plays a good game quickly, by rules such as taking the largest number that can still be taken, or the one that costs the least tax. Many heuristic strategies have been proposed, and judging different heuristics benefits from having the best score possible to measure against. That is one reason to know opt(n). The repository holds our strategies and how they were measured, and Robert Moniot's seven.

The blue button plays an optimal game, the best you can do. For game 10 you can find one by hand. Playing well is easy even in large games: a simple strategy with no search comes within a fraction of a percent of the best score. But only a perfect game is optimal, and only a proof shows that a game is perfect.

How we did it

The counts

Rough sizes, not counts of legal games: n! move orders, 2n subsets of the board, 2⌊n/2⌋ subsets of the numbers up to n/2, and 2c subsets of the c candidates. The last row is the nodes that the game's recorded search explored. The row before it is no count: it is where the search starts, as a score below the best, from the game before and from a start of its own, measured (chapter 5).

Game n's numbers can be put in about 10… orders. No computer could try them all. Each chapter of the story takes away part of the problem, until what is left is small enough for a search to finish.

The chain

How each game was proven

Each game counts in the first group that fits it.

  • Derived from game n − 1. n is prime, and the prime formula gives its score from game n − 1’s. Or it is at its ceiling: its score is n + opt(n − 1), the most it can be.
  • Repeats an earlier search. The game poses the same search problem as an earlier game, and that game’s answer is its answer too.
  • Search. Every other game. A search proved its score.

The … small games with nothing left to choose count as searches of one node.

The time of a search

Each column is the median time of a search in its stretch of 100 games: half the stretch’s searches took less time, and half took more.

  • It counts every search the chain ran, … in all, even the few that ended at the ceiling.
  • A search’s time is the time it had at least one thread at work.
  • One run on one machine of 88 threads made the whole chain. It worked on up to four games at once, and a search’s time counts the time it shared the threads with the others.
The player's share

The share is the player’s score, opt(n), divided by the total of the board, 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n.

In 1988 Hensley proved that as n grows, the share settles toward one value, somewhere between 50% and about 73%. The chain’s best scores put it near 63%. That is a measurement, not a proof.

We solved the games in order, from game 1 up, each from an optimal game of the one before, so they form a chain. The chart shows every game of it: the share of the board that the player wins, how each game was proven, and how long a search took.
