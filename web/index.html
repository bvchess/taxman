Taxman

Playing optimal games

Brian Chess

Taxman began as a number game to teach kids about factors. We play an optimal game, one with the best possible score, in each of games 1 to 5,060. This site explains how we did it.

In a hurry? Go straight to the summary.

The game

The rules, precisely

Each move the player makes takes a number that still has a proper divisor in the pot, then the taxman takes all of its divisors still in the pot. When no move remains, the taxman takes the rest. The best player score possible for game n is opt(n), and opt(1), opt(2), opt(3), … is the Taxman sequence, A019312 in the OEIS.

You play Taxman with the numbers 1 to n, which start in the pot.

  • Take a number from the pot and score it. That number is your move.
  • The taxman then takes every divisor of your move still in the pot: its tax.
  • You may take only a number that has a divisor left in the pot, so the taxman always gets something.
  • When no number you could take remains, the taxman takes the rest.
  • You beat the taxman when your total is higher than the total he collects.

We write the best total you can reach in game n as opt(n), and a game that reaches it is an optimal game. Try game 10: its best score is 40, and you can find it by hand.

Heuristic strategies

A heuristic strategy plays a good game quickly, by rules such as taking the largest number you can still take, or the one that costs the least tax. To judge one, you need the best score to measure it against: one reason to know opt(n). The repository holds our strategies and how we measured them, and Robert Moniot's seven.

The blue button plays an optimal game, the best you can do. Playing well is easy: in every game we tested it on, the first thousand, a simple strategy with no search comes within a fraction of a percent of the best score. But knowing that a game is optimal means showing that no game does better, and that is more challenging.

How we did it

The counts

Rough sizes, not counts of legal games: n! move orders, 2n subsets of the board, 2⌊n/2⌋ subsets of the numbers up to n/2, and 2c subsets of the c candidates. The last row is the nodes that the game's recorded search explored. The row before it is no count: it says how far below the best score the search starts when it starts from the game before, as we measured it (chapter 5).

You can put game n's numbers in about 10… orders. No computer could try them all. Each chapter of the story takes away part of the problem, until what remains is small enough for a search to finish.

The chain

How we proved each score

Each game counts in the first group that fits it.

  • Follows from game n − 1. n is prime, and the prime formula gives its score from game n − 1’s. Or it is at its ceiling: its score is n + opt(n − 1), the most it can be.
  • Repeats an earlier search. The game poses the same search problem as an earlier game, and that game’s answer is its answer too.
  • Search. Every other game. A search proved its score.

The … small games with nothing left to choose count as searches of one node.

The time of a search

The line passes through the median time of a search in each stretch of 100 games: half the stretch’s searches took less time, and half took more. All … searches ran on one machine with 88 threads.

The player's share

The share is the player’s score, opt(n), over the total of the board, 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n.

In 1988 Hensley proved that as n grows, the share settles toward one value, somewhere between 50% and about 73%. The chain’s best scores put it near 63%. That is a measurement, not a proof.

We solved the games in order, from game 1 up, so that each game can take advantage of the one before. The games form a chain. The chart shows every game of it: the share of the board that the player wins, how we proved each score, and how long a search took.
