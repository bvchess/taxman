Notation

What the marks, colors, pills and symbols of this site mean. Every figure and every page uses the same ones.

The page

Every page has three columns. The navigation is on the left. The story is in the center, and we write it so that you can read it on its own. The right column has what is more exact than the story, beside the paragraph it belongs to: the precise statement of each result, technical notes, and links to the code, the data and the literature.

On a narrow screen the right column folds into the text, and each of its notes opens when you tap it.

The marks

Every figure draws numbers with the same marks. A circle is a source, a number above n/2, or any number the player takes as a move; a square is a factor, a number up to n/2. Until the story tells sources from factors, at the sources of chapter 3, a number is plain, a rounded square, neither one nor the other. The rest of each mark says what has happened to it.

Flat or raised. A number you can play stands up from the page as a token: a coin for a circle, a tile for a square, and a rounded tile for a plain number. A number the player cannot take lies flat. The numbers in a figure that explains something lie flat, in ink. So depth means you can touch it.

Layers and frames. A figure that sets a game out by layers puts each layer in a column, with layer 1 at the right and the highest layer at the left, so every line runs right, toward the frames a game plays first. In a column the larger numbers stand higher, except where a figure orders a column so that its lines run straight or do not cross. A box around its sources and its factors stands for a frame, and a dashed box for a closed frame. We number the frames with freedoms from the front. Frame 0 is at the right, frame 1 is behind it, and so on, and we collapse frames at the same distance from the front into one. A dotted line joins a candidate to each of its maximal factors in the frame in front, and only a promotion, the candidate's hop into that frame, wears an arrowhead.

In the text. A number in bold with a dotted underline lights up in the figure beside it when you point at it. A small tag moves the figure to the state it names, after an underlined phrase or, in a list of steps, on its own.

Color

Three colors mean something, one for each actor in the story. Blue is you, the player: your moves and your score. Orange is the taxman and his tax. Green is the search, the computer's work that proves a game: the games a search settled, and what each search cost. Every other mark is ink and gray. A shape, a place or a label backs each color too, so a reader who cannot tell the colors apart misses nothing.

Where a result stands

Each result in the right column wears a pill that says where it stands. The pill is a link to the result's proof, or to the evidence for it.

proved
The theory proves it.
conditional
The theory proves it from statement U, which is open in general.
proved by computation
A statement about particular games, which we prove by running a program on them.
open, proved to game N
An open statement that a computation proves in every game up to game N.
measured
The data show it, with no proof.
open
No one has proved or refuted it.

A card gives a result's name and its label in the theory, such as Lemma P1, then the result in plain words, and below a thin line its exact statement.
