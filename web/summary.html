The optimal games

For every game from game 1 to game 5,060, we give a sequence of moves that reaches the best possible score, and say how we know that no sequence scores more. Up to game 2,530 that is proven outright; past it, it rests on statement U, which is open (chapter 6). The best scores, opt(1), opt(2), …, are the Taxman sequence, A019312 in the OEIS, and results/b019312.txt holds them in the OEIS's own format.

One file of 1.1 MB, results/optimal.jsonl.gz, has a line for each game: its best score, a sequence of moves that reaches it, in the order played, and how we know no sequence scores more. The explorer of chapter 7 shows any game, and its last section says what each field holds. These commands download the file and take out the moves; they need jq.

Moves that reach the best score of game 21, in the order played (change 21 to any game)
curl -sL https://github.com/bvchess/taxman/raw/master/results/optimal.jsonl.gz | gzip -dc | jq -c 'select(.n == 21) | .moves'
Every game, a line each: n, its best score, and moves that reach it
curl -sL https://github.com/bvchess/taxman/raw/master/results/optimal.jsonl.gz | gzip -dc | jq -r '"\(.n) \(.score) \(.moves | join(" "))"' > games.txt

Game 21, step by step

Game 20's best score is 124, so game 21's is above 124 and at most its ceiling, 124 + 21 = 145 (chapter 2). 21 does not fit and is not a prime, so game 20 does not settle game 21 at once. Rules settle most of the board, and the figure follows them one step at a time.

  1. The pot The numbers 1 to 21, none of them sorted yet.
  2. Layers Each number stands in the column of its count of prime factors, with a line to each of its maximal factors, the numbers that can pay for it (chapter 1).
  3. Sources The numbers above n/2, 11 to 21, divide nothing else on the board, so each is a move or nothing. They are the sources, and the numbers up to 10 are the factors that pay for them (chapter 3).
  4. Descending greedy From 21 down, descending greedy keeps each source still playable with those kept before it: 21, 20, 19, 18, 16, 15, 14 and 12, which score 135. It drops 17, 13 and 11, whose only maximal factor, 1, already pays for 19. By statement U, which holds here, some optimal game plays every source greedy keeps (chapter 3).
  5. Frames What is left splits into frames, each a group of sources with the factors that can pay for them (chapter 4).
  6. Freedoms The two frames at the ends have as many factors as moves, so nothing in them can change, and they drop out. The other two have one freedom each, frame 0 at the right and frame 1 behind it, and their candidates, the factors that could be played as moves too, are 4 and 9 (chapter 4). No earlier game poses this problem, so game 21 is not a repeat, and it needs a search (chapter 5).
  7. The promotion Only one candidate fits, and 9 is the larger, so 9 is promoted from frame 1: it becomes a move, paid for by 3. The search over promotions ends at its first node: 135 + 9 = 144 (chapter 4 and chapter 5).
  8. The game Played in this order, the moves are 19, 9, 15, 21, 14, 18, 12, 20, 16, each paid for by its tax. The player scores 144, and the taxman 87.

So game 21's best score is 144, one below its ceiling. Two programs that share no code check it with every other game, and the prover proves it again on the whole board, without statement U (chapter 6).
