Chapter 1

A game is a set

The set of numbers you pick decides your score. The order of moves follows from the set, and whether a set is playable is quick to check.

Your score is the sum of the numbers you take, so it does not depend on the order you take them in. The order matters only to keep every move legal: each number you take must still have a divisor in the pot.

Here are the nine moves required for optimal play in game 21. You can play them in several orders, and in every one of them each move takes the same tax. Step through them.

Sets of moves

A set S ⊆ {2, …, n} is playable when some order of it is the moves of a legal game of n. Its weight is ΣS, the sum of its members. An optimal set, which we write S*(n), is the set of moves of an optimal game: a heaviest playable set. Some games can have more than one (game 425 is the first), so we say an optimal set (the theory's notation).

So the question for game n is not how to play but what to take. We call a set of numbers playable when some order of it is a legal game. An optimal set is a playable set with the largest sum, the set of moves of an optimal game.

Tax as payment

The lifting lemmaLemma P1proved, proof on GitHub

You can play a number if and only if one of its maximal factors is still in the pot.

Precisely.The maximal factors of x are x/p, for each prime p that divides x. At any point of a game, if a proper divisor d of x is in the pot, so is a maximal factor f of x with d | f.

A move is legal when one of its divisors is still in the pot, and only some divisors matter: a number's maximal factors, what you get when you divide the number by one of its primes. 10 has the maximal factors 5 and 2, 8 has only 4, though 2 divides it too, and a prime has only 1. If any divisor of a move is still in the pot, so is one of its maximal factors. So a move is legal exactly when a maximal factor of it is still in the pot.

Think of that factor as what pays for the move. Two moves cannot share a payment, because whichever you take first sends the factor to the taxman. So the player can play a set only if each of its numbers has a maximal factor of its own, from outside the set: a number you take as a move cannot also pay for another.

The graph

The maximal-factor graph of game n is a directed graph, with an edge from each number x to each of its maximal factors. Every edge leads to a smaller number, so the figures draw the edges as lines without arrowheads; in the board below, every edge runs one column to the right. The divisors of x are exactly the numbers that x reaches along its edges. The columns are the numbers' layers.

As the game goes on, each number the player takes leaves the graph with its edges, so the board shows the graph of the numbers still in the pot. By the lifting lemma, x still reaches each of its divisors left in the pot through numbers left in the pot. So the tax of a move is everything it reaches on the board, and a move is legal exactly when it still has an outgoing edge.

The graph is the Hasse diagram of divisibility on {1, …, n}: d is directly below x exactly when x/d is prime.

Draw every number joined to its maximal factors, and the game becomes much easier to play. The board below is that maximal-factor graph. Its columns count prime factors: the primes stand in the column at the right, 1 beyond them, and each number sits one column to the left of its maximal factors, so every line runs to the right. Taking a number sends the taxman everything it reaches by following its lines to the right, and every number the player takes leaves the graph with its lines. A number is a legal move, and stands up, as long as it still has a line to its right.

The maximal-factor graph makes it easy to see what a move will cost before you make it, as well as what it will leave for the moves after it. That makes the game far easier than playing on the plain board shown in the introduction.

The playability theoremTheorem P2proved, proof on GitHub

A set is playable exactly when each member has a maximal factor of its own to pay for it, from outside the set, with no circle in what must come first.

Precisely.S ⊆ {2, …, n} is playable iff it has a payment scheme, a one-to-one choice of a maximal factor outside S for each member, whose precedence is acyclic. Then every topological order of the precedence is a legal game.

Franklín and Moniot

Their Theorem 1 says the same in the language of matchings: a set is playable iff it is the set of upper ends of a matching of the maximal-factor graph with no flat alternating cycle (the paper; how the theory relates to it).

Designating a payment for each number is not quite enough, because the order can still tie itself in a knot. A move takes all of its divisors in the pot, not only the one that pays for it, so it can take a factor that another move was counting on. In game 10, 3 pays for 9, and 6 would take 3 as tax, so 9 must come before 6. Then 2 pays for 6, but 8 and 10 would take 2, so 6 must come before them. A move must also come before any move it divides: 5 before 10, if a player wanted to take both, or 10 would take 5.

Rules like these say which moves must come before which. A set of moves is playable exactly when you can give each move a maximal factor of its own to pay with, in a way whose rules never run in a circle: each move before the next, and the last before the first. Then any order that keeps the rules is a legal game.

Peel

Trying every way to pay would be laborious. A quicker test never has to guess. It pairs numbers with their payments one at a time, using only two kinds of safe step:

  • A number with only one maximal factor left pays with it.
  • A factor that only one remaining number can use pays for that number.

Each step takes a pair out, and the next step looks at what remains. We call this a peel test. If it pairs every number, the set is playable. If the steps run out with numbers left over, it is not.

The peel theoremTheorem P4proved, proof on GitHub

Peel decides whether a set is playable, whichever safe steps it takes in whichever order.

Precisely.A front move pairs a number that has one factor left to pay with; a back move pairs a factor that one number alone can still use. Each leaves a playable set playable and an unplayable set unplayable. A set is playable iff a run of peel pairs every number, and the pairs then form an acyclic payment scheme.

Peel in the code

In game 21, peel pairs 19 with 1, the only factor it has, and 9 with 3 for the same reason. Once 3 is gone, 15 has only 5 left and 21 only 7, and the rest follow. The game can use the pairs it finds as its payments.

A run of peel that pairs every number also reveals a legal order of play: play each number before any move that would take it, or its payment, as tax. So peel quickly turns a playable set into an ordered list of moves.

Loop trouble

When peel gets stuck on numbers with two maximal factors, a loop is to blame. Take the numbers 10, 14, 15 and 21, with only the primes 2, 3, 5 and 7 left to pay with. Each of the four is the product of two primes, which are its maximal factors, so in the maximal-factor graph it has a line to each of two primes. Unfold the graph, putting each number between its two primes, and the loop appears: the four make a square, 10 = 2 · 5, 15 = 5 · 3, 21 = 3 · 7 and 14 = 7 · 2. Every number has two ways to pay, and every prime can pay for two numbers, so peel has no safe step. Try to play all four.

The loop ruleProposition P11proved, proof on GitHub

Numbers that each have two maximal factors outside their set make a playable set exactly when they and those factors make no loop.

Precisely.An instance whose members each list two factors is playable iff its lists are linearly independent over GF(2), iff its graph, which joins each member to the two factors it lists, is a forest. With members that list one factor, the same holds when the graph also joins each of them to one more vertex, the ground. A member that lists none can never be played.

Whatever order you choose, the first number takes both of its primes as tax. That leaves two primes for the other three numbers, and one of them is always stuck.

Every loop gets stuck the same way. A loop of k numbers has k factors among them. No matter which number you choose to play first, the taxman will take both of its factors, and that will leave at most k − 2 factors for the other k − 1 numbers.

A loop is also the only way peel gets stuck on sets of numbers with two maximal factors to pay with. Peel is stuck when every number left still has two factors and every factor still has two numbers. Then every point has a second line out, so a walk along the lines that never turns back must come around to a point it passed: a loop.

So for numbers with two maximal factors, whether they can all be played is something you can see: draw their lines and look for a loop. Above n/2 that picture settles one part of an important choice: which numbers with two prime factors every optimal game must play (chapter 3).

The length propositionProposition Q4proved by computation, proof on GitHub

In game 833 every optimal game has 362 moves, and the longest games have 363.

Precisely.In game 833 = 7² · 17 a heaviest game of 363 moves scores 218,197, which is 29 less than opt(833) = 218,226. The prover and a maximum matching compute it.

Along the way: game 833 OpenClose

Taking more numbers does not always score more. In game 833, every optimal game makes 362 moves, yet some games make one more. The best of those still scores less, and one of them shows why: it plays five smaller numbers where the optimal game plays four larger ones.

The trade happens below n/2. Each of the nine numbers is a maximal factor of a number the optimal game plays, and a number the player takes as a move cannot also pay for another. The optimal game takes the four large ones and pays for those numbers some other way. That longest game leaves the four to pay, and takes five smaller ones instead.
