To our knowledge, these are original to this work:

  • Optimal scores

    For every game from game 1 to game n, a sequence of moves that reaches the best score, which we find from the game before, and how we know, and under what assumption, that no sequence scores more. Up to game 2,531 the prover proves the scores on the whole board too, without the frame model, so they assume no open statement (what supports the scores; chapter 8).

  • From one game to the nextproved, proof on GitHub

    Three formulas take an optimal game of n − 1 to an optimal game of n with no search: the step theorem, where n fits into game n − 1's optimal set and the score reaches its ceiling, the prime formula, and the semiprime formula. Each step assumes nothing. Together they settle 65% of games 2 to 5,060, a share that holds in every thousand games (chapter 2).

  • The top halfproved in layers 1 and 2, proof on GitHub

    Descending greedy, one pass from the largest number down, chooses the numbers above n/2 that an optimal game plays. The bottom-layers theorem proves it in layers 1 and 2, where every optimal game plays one prime, the largest, and among the numbers above n/2 with two prime factors exactly those that descending greedy keeps. Above layer 2 it holds given statement U, which the prover proves in every game up to 2,531 (chapter 3).

  • The frame modelproved, proof on GitHub

    The frame model leaves only the promotions to choose, and the exactness theorem makes its best score the best score of a game that plays U*, which is opt(n) when statement U holds (chapter 4).

  • The parity boundproved, proof on GitHub

    An upper bound on opt(n) from linear algebra over GF(2). Measured, it is much tighter than Franklín and Moniot’s, and equals opt(n) in every game up to 77 (chapter 6).

Other results:

  • The peel theoremproved, proof on GitHub

    Peel decides whether a set of moves is playable, and never has to guess.

  • The counting theoremproved, proof on GitHub

    A count of payment schemes that places the parity bound between the playable sets and Franklín and Moniot’s bound (chapter 6).

  • The findings along the way

    Two optimal sets at game 425, an optimal game one move short of the longest at game 833, and the others, a card each.

The game. Taxman is an educational computer game of the Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium, which published it in a collection of mathematics games and drills in 1983, as Hensley’s paper records.

The Taxman sequence. The best scores opt(1), opt(2), opt(3), … are the Taxman sequence, A019312 in the OEIS. The OEIS credits its earlier terms to Dan Hoey, up to game 158; Timothy Loh, up to game 227; Bernhard Esslinger, up to game 404; and van Nek, up to game 519. The terms they computed agree with the chain’s, one for one. We added the terms from game 520 to game 1,000 with an earlier solver of our own, so those are not an independent check.

The share. Douglas Hensley proved in 1988 that the player’s share of the board tends to a constant, more than 1/2 and at most 35/48 (chapter 8). His strategy splits the board into blocks that the player plays in turn, each without disturbing the blocks after it.

Playability and hardness. Atli Fannar Franklín and Robert K. Moniot proved in 2023 the playability theorem of chapter 1, in the language of matchings in the maximal-factor graph. They bounded opt(n) above by the heaviest matching of that graph, and proved NP-hard a more general game, on any set of things that sit in levels. Whether Taxman itself is that hard is open.

Heuristic strategies. A heuristic strategy plays a good game quickly, with no proof. Robert Moniot’s program plays seven, and the best of them, OneTax, wins 99.065% of the optimal points over games 2 to 1,000 (his strategies, and his published comparison of them). The repository keeps the program as he sent it. Its source cites three earlier articles on the game: by L. A. Carmony and R. L. Holliday, 1993; by J. Trono, 1994; and by R. Moniot, 2007. The repository’s own strategies win 99.85% of the optimal points up to game 1,000, with no search (how we measured them).

How to cite the site

Cite the site by its author, title and address, and the code and the data by the repository:

Brian Chess. Taxman: Playing optimal games. https://vantuyl.com/taxman. Code and data: https://github.com/bvchess/taxman.

A score is best cited with the results file and its commit. The numbers of this site come from results/optimal.jsonl.gz at commit ….
